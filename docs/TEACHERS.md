# First Outpost in the classroom

A guide for teachers and parents. The game is free, runs in any modern browser (phones, tablets, laptops), needs no accounts, stores nothing outside the device, and after the first visit works without a connection. Play it at <https://first-outpost.vercel.app>. It is in English and Russian.

## What children learn

Each idea is something the child does in the game, not something they only read.

| Idea | Where it happens in the game | What to listen for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Engineering means trade-offs.** There is never enough for everything, so you set priorities and accept a cost. | Every week the player shares a few energy cells between life support, the greenhouse, the lab and the radiation shield. The plan screen shows what the week will do before they commit. | "If I give the lab more, the air goes down." |
| **People in space need air, water and food made or kept on site.** | Life support cleans the air and recycles water; the greenhouse grows food (radishes take about 27 days, lettuce about 33, as on the Space Station). The water recycler in the living module tells how the Space Station gets back about 98% of its water. | "The plants need power too." |
| **Space radiation is dangerous, and shielding helps.** | The builder robot piles regolith into a wall around the home. Real solar storms from NASA's records arrive on their real dates; the player chooses whether to shelter or keep working, and a radiation budget shows the dose. | "We hid in the bunker because of the storm." |
| **Light takes time to travel.** Messages to the Moon take about 1.3 seconds; to Mars, from about 3 to 22 minutes each way. | The forecast shows the real radio delay for the mission's dates. Asking Mission Control on Mars can mean the answer comes too late, so the crew decides on its own. | "Earth can't help us fast, so we have to choose." |
| **Gravity is different on other worlds.** Mars 3.71 m/s², Moon 1.62 m/s², Earth 9.81 m/s². | Walks outside use each world's real gravity (high, slow jumps on the Moon). The landing simulator in the lander uses it too, and its result card compares the three worlds. | "On the Moon I jump much higher." |
| **Explorers use what they find.** Using local material (regolith, metal) saves carrying everything from Earth. | Regolith and metal dug on walks become new buildings and tools; the workshop prints tools one layer at a time. Real stories: NASA's 3D-printed habitat challenge, the first 3D printer in space (2014). | "We built the oxygen plant from moon rock." |
| **Real data and simplified numbers are not the same thing.** | The forecast marks which values are real records (with the source); the game's own rules and numbers are listed as simplifications. | "That temperature is really from Curiosity." |

## A 40-minute lesson

1. **Start (5 min).** Ask: what would people need to live on Mars or the Moon for six weeks? Collect answers (air, water, food, power, protection, talking to home).
2. **Play together (10 min).** On a projector, start a new mission on Mars (this link opens one right away: <https://first-outpost.vercel.app/?play=may2024&world=mars>). Watch the one-minute opening film (it explains the goal and the four steps of every week). Read the first forecast aloud. Let the class vote on how to share the energy cells, then run the week and read the report together.
3. **Play in pairs (15 min).** Pairs continue for two or three weeks. One child plays, the other explains each choice out loud; swap every week. Give the task: keep air, food and the radiation budget safe, and earn as much science as you can.
4. **Talk (10 min).** Use the questions below. If a pair finished a mission, look at its review screen together: it says which choice caused what.

For a longer project, let groups compare Mars and the Moon, or the same mission at two different dates (the storm of May 2024 is a good one; the radio delay changes with the planets' positions).

## Questions for the talk

1. Which system did you give the fewest cells, and what happened because of it?
2. In the storm week, did you shelter or keep working? What did each choice cost?
3. On Mars a message to Earth can take 20 minutes each way. Who should make decisions on a Mars base, and why?
4. Why do astronauts recycle almost all of their water instead of bringing new water?
5. The game simplified many things (one turn is one week, energy comes in whole cells). Find one simplification. Why do you think the game made it?

## Connections to standards

The activities connect to these US Next Generation Science Standards performance expectations (check the exact wording for your grade before you plan):

- **3-5-ETS1-1, 3-5-ETS1-2** — define a design problem with criteria and constraints; compare solutions (sharing energy cells, choosing buildings).
- **MS-ETS1-1, MS-ETS1-2** — criteria and constraints of a design problem; evaluate competing solutions (the power plan and its preview).
- **4-PS4-3** — compare solutions that use patterns to send information (the radio and its delay; the radio game in the lander).
- **5-PS3-1** — the energy in food was once energy from the Sun (the greenhouse runs on solar power).
- **MS-LS1-6** — the role of photosynthesis in the cycling of matter and flow of energy (plants make food and oxygen).
- **MS-ESS1-3** — scale properties of objects in the solar system (distances, radio delay, gravity on each world).

## Reading help and access

- **Voices:** the films and Lumi's most frequent lines are spoken by synthetic voices (made with ElevenLabs from the game's own texts). Settings → Voices turns them off.
- **Read-aloud:** a speaker button on Lumi's lines and on every card; it can read new texts by itself (Settings).
- **Text size:** three sizes (Settings).
- **Colour-blind help:** meters and chart lines can use patterns as well as colours.
- **Calm mode:** a camera with no shake and no automatic turning.
- **Words:** science words are underlined and explain themselves on tap.
- **Controls:** keyboard and mouse, or touch (a stick and big buttons), with a left-handed layout for phones; a gamepad works on walks and in the rooms.

## Good to know

- **Time:** one week of a mission takes about 3 to 5 minutes; a whole 6-week mission about 20 to 30 minutes. Progress is saved on the device, so a mission can be continued in the next lesson.
- **Failure is part of it:** a failed mission ends with an early flight home, a clear reason and "try again". Nothing is lost.
- **"Why?" buttons:** the forecast, the event card, the power plan, the walk briefing and the weekly report each have a "Why?" button. It replays a short film about the task and ends on a card: what to do, why it matters, and a true fact with its source. Good for starting a class discussion.
- **Playing together (optional):** "Play with friends" lets up to four pupils fly the very same mission (same real Sun, same cards) and compare their crews at the end. It needs an internet connection, has no chat and no typed names (only eight preset stickers), and only game numbers travel between the devices. Details: [MULTIPLAYER.md](MULTIPLAYER.md). A crew mission replaces a solo mission saved on that device.
- **Facts:** every fact the game tells has a source; the Science log cards link to the NASA pages they come from. The checks are in [docs/sources/](sources/), and what the game simplified is listed in [NASA_DATA.md](NASA_DATA.md).
- **Not an official NASA product.** The game uses NASA's open data and images with their credits; NASA does not endorse it.
